Not wanting to use AI and not knowing how are two different things. It's the former I don't talk to. The people who don't want to, the ones who resist it at their core, will never get it no matter how much you say, so I'd rather not engage, and I certainly won't go out of my way to convince them. People who don't know how are different: they haven't found a use case yet, but they want to learn and are willing to try. If you're the latter, I'm happy to talk and share how I use AI myself.

More than ninety percent of what I do, in life and at work, runs through AI. Whenever something needs doing, my first reaction is no longer "how do I do this" but "can AI do this" and "can this be automated." That reaction is the default now. I don't have to remind myself.

I've solved a lot of problems with AI, and I've used it to build tools and set up automated workflows that make my work more efficient. Once the technical bar drops, what you can do is on a different order of magnitude. That's the most effective way to solve problems: not training yourself into a more efficient executor, but handing execution itself away.

There's a third kind of person, stuck between the two: they tried it, didn't like the output, and went back to doing everything by hand. But bad output, nine times out of ten, means you used it wrong, not that it can't do the job. I get disappointing output all the time. I don't just give up. I diagnose what went wrong, adjust, and keep iterating until it works. Treating one unsatisfying result as a final verdict is about as irresponsible as judgments get. These people aren't refusing to use it. They don't know how, and think they do. As for the ones who wrote it off without ever trying it: that's pure refusal. Like I said at the start, we have nothing to talk about.

To put it bluntly: for the people who won't use it, being left behind is only a matter of time.
